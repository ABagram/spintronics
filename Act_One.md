## Tutorial 1: The battery

Spintronic circuits are powered by a **battery**. The battery pulls chains through circuits, just like electrical batteries pull electrons through wires.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/nnQn38z.png" alt="Image" width="70%" /></div>

**Pull the string back and let it fly!** It got going too fast, didn't it?

Good thing I built a circuit breaker into it!

Try it again. This time, after you pull back the string, **use your fingers to add some resistance to the output sprockets**. Can you release all of the energy in the battery without triggering the circuit breaker?

From now on, we'll have to avoid **shorts**! A short is wherever the battery has a path to release its energy without any resistance. How do you add resistance to the battery without using your fingers? With a resistor.

## Tutorial 3: Circuits

Electronic circuits are always made of loops. A power station doesn't **make** electrons and send them to a house through wires, it simply pushes and pulls the electrons that are already in the wires.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/F1WHiyq.png" alt="Image" width="70%" /></div>

It's the same with spintronics. The spintronic battery doesn't **make** chain, it simply pulls a loop of chain around a circuit.

**What happens if you don't make a loop?**

In spintronics, the chain flies out onto the floor, and it eventually stops running. Luckily, in electronics, the electrons don't spill out onto the floor. That would leave a very dangerous situation in your house, and the power station would run out of electrons to push!

## Tutorial 4: Chain Tension

Spintronic circuits are "analog." What does that mean? Well, things that are analog are **continuous**. For example, take these graphs:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/PVwbnLi.png" alt="Image" width="70%" /></div>

Things that are analog could have an **infinite number of possible values** (like the graph on the left). The opposite of analog is "digital." Things that are digital can only have **certain values** (like the graph on the right).

Analog is pretty great, right?! The tricky part about things that are analog is that **every little imperfection** affects it. For example, the speed a spintronic resistor turns is affected by things like the temperature, air resistance, humidity, physical imperfections, and other things you can't easily control.

Eventually, you'll learn ways to design circuits that can **tolerate** imperfections, but for now, we're going to focus on one important thing that you can control: **<u>chain tension</u>**.

Connect two switches together with chain, like this:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//e6fb7cb1-b7d1-4156-aa72-88042cd255d4/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_419_252_746_552.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2Fccaf6e32f19ac45134ea19d7b07bd29f8b9d7551f31b0d8038afed1a349193a1" alt="Image" width="27%" /></div>

## Tutorial 5: Current

How fast does electricity flow through wires? Incredibly fast, right?! NOPE! Electrons move more like the chains in spintronics.

How is that possible? Well imagine a long tube full of marbles. If you push a marble into one end, a marble pops out the other end immediately, even though the marbles aren't moving very fast.
<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/8otYgN7.png" alt="Image" width="80%" /></div>

It's the same with electrons in wires. Signals travel fast through wires, but only because the wires are already full of electrons. Spintronic chains do this, too! If you pull one end of a long chain, the other end moves immediately, even though the chains aren't moving very fast.
<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/9qefx6b.png" width="70%" /></div>

The movement of chain (or electrons) through a circuit is called "current." A fast moving chain has high current. Current is measured in spin amps. The symbol for a spin amp is "A."

## Tutorial 15: Kirchoff's Law

This is one of those things that's obvious once you understand it, but is hard figure it out on your own. Ready? Here it is: **In any circuit loop, the sum of all the voltages is zero.**

What does that mean? Well, take a look at this circuit:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/eziPNFB.png" alt="Image" width="38%" /></div>


The battery produces 6 V, and it pushes chain through a loop with three resistors. Each of those resistors push back against the current. That "push back" is also a voltage - a **negative voltage**! Bigger resistors generate more negative voltage than smaller resistors. Add all the voltages in the loop together and what do you get? Zero spin volts!

There's a term for the negative voltage generated by each resistor. It's called the "**voltage drop**". That's because the voltage drops by that amount from one side of the resistor to the other. In the next picture, I drew in the voltage at each point in the circuit.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//8b6e3855-2548-4111-b368-31aa6d762a3b/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_288_223_710_624.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2F80dd569c8cc974793a56406fe630b4547b758d79720da79ca94b0ffb4cf87c90" alt="Image" width="34%" /></div>


Ok, so now that you know about voltage drop, how do you measure it? You've got to measure the **difference in voltage** from one side of the part to the other: the voltage **across** the part. Let's measure the voltage drop across the 200 Ω resistor.

Can you do it like this, in series?

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//8b6e3855-2548-4111-b368-31aa6d762a3b/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_535_892_951_1241.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2Faaf189c806967225b3bd29035cc8634949c6c581da95bd347a0a4b4d39913b1f" alt="Image" width="33%" /></div>

**Nope!** The capacitor doesn't let current through. Once it charges, it becomes the biggest resistance in the whole loop and then you're just measuring the voltage across it!

To measure the voltage across a part, you've got to put the capacitor (voltmeter) **in parallel** with the part. Like this:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/qvGXJvD.png"
 width="60%" /></div>

At first, this was confusing for me. Doesn't the capacitor create another path for current? It doesn't really. The capacitor charges up quickly and then blocks any more current from flowing through that path. It just charges to the **difference in voltage** from point A to point B. That is, it charges to **the voltage across the 200 Ω resistor**.

This still might be hard to understand, so try this. Build the circuit above, but take out the capacitor, like this:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//3a9f314f-7449-4050-a25c-42b98851d62a/markdown_1/imgs/img_in_image_box_285_350_711_786.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A32Z%2F-1%2F%2F0ce4b289a587278d134f6103c7a8f9b29abac2d9f560ea03b8b78550533b7421" alt="Image" width="34%" /></div>

Now, **hold the top sprocket** of the junction still with your fingers while you run the circuit. Remember, when one sprocket of a junction is held still, the junction behaves like **nothing more than a series connection!** All the current going in one sprocket goes out the other sprocket.

In the remaining puzzles, we're going to explore some unique things you can do with spintronics, but can't do with electronics. These useful tricks can often help you make simpler circuits!

Now you might have already noticed that **junctions don't always turn the way you'd expect**. For example:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//d5ce1f0d-0d17-4a55-a839-c09b5c68c62e/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_138_571_487_1054.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A39Z%2F-1%2F%2F0c81cfb63a8e3d80737e5ae24eb2c902dc9b58b894276f783b48fec2dfdd817b" alt="Image" width="29%" /></div>

In this circuit, the battery turns clockwise and so do R1 and R2.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//d5ce1f0d-0d17-4a55-a839-c09b5c68c62e/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_621_570_989_1056.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A40Z%2F-1%2F%2F6d8dccd2f2ea112a3c6e5db788fdeef3b031c5915476569e5b721167b23d7d02" alt="Image" width="30%" /></div>

In this circuit, the battery and R1 turn clockwise, **but R2 turns counterclockwise.**

The difference is that in the first circuit, the battery applies voltage to the bottom sprocket, and in the second circuit, the battery applies voltage to the middle sprocket.

If you apply voltage to one sprocket of a junction, how do you know which way it will push the other sprockets? Here's how you can figure it out:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/T0dN1ig.png" alt="Image" width="69%" /></div>

First, look at the picture and find the sprocket you are applying a voltage to (like with the battery). Then follow the arrows to the other sprockets. If it's a green arrow, it will push that sprocket with the **same** voltage. If it's a red arrow, it will push it with the **opposite** voltage.

For example, if you apply a voltage to the middle sprocket, it pushes on the bottom sprocket in the **same** direction, but it pushes on the top sprocket in the **opposite** direction. Or if you apply a voltage to the bottom sprocket, it pushes on the middle sprocket in the same direction and the top sprocket in the **same** direction.

## Tutorial 18: Spintronic Coupling

Most spintronic parts have three sprockets that are joined together. Take the resistor, for instance. All three sprockets are joined, so that if one moves, the other two also move. We say that **chains attached to joined sprockets are "coupled" together.**

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//522f3264-ab6b-4121-850a-79aaef04f5ec/markdown_1/imgs/img_in_image_box_418_447_663_760.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2Ffea5c0e0015d974251c61f04817e21913869344ffc426a7de73daaf660c2dbe1" alt="Image" width="20%" /></div>

It might not seem like much, but there's nothing quite like that in electronics, and it's **powerful**. Eventually you'll see why.

First, let's figure out how to diagram "coupled" chains. Here's a series circuit with two resistors:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//522f3264-ab6b-4121-850a-79aaef04f5ec/markdown_1/imgs/img_in_image_box_322_1082_781_1478.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2F9fc715c5b197bbc299d62112bb6c8ad7595c822a583954896ee33c7832194c42" alt="Image" width="38%" /></div>

And here's how we've been drawing the circuit diagram for that circuit:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//522f3264-ab6b-4121-850a-79aaef04f5ec/markdown_2/imgs/img_in_image_box_575_173_940_425.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A33Z%2F-1%2F%2F3c42819676eb62a93f50f02099e4e5015158db792b7eb423d1f99721e9bef4ff" alt="Image" width="30%" /></div>

But technically, this is the correct way to draw it:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/7QVRD0C.png" alt="Image" width="60%" /></div>

The top chain loop turns because it's "coupled" to the bottom chain loop at the 1000 Ω resistor. That dashed line between the 1000 Ω resistor and the top loop tells us that **both loops wrap around that resistor**. Of course, that means the current in each loop **must be the same**.

Now, do you see those two dots next to the coupling? Since they're both on the **same end** of the coupling, they tell us that both chains turn the **same direction** around the resistor (clockwise).

Now you're probably thinking, "Are you crazy? Why not just draw it like the first diagram - it's waууy simpler!" Well, yeah, in this case, the first diagram is much simpler, and you can just draw it that way, but sometimes you have to draw the coupling.

For instance, here the two loops are **coupled oppositely**. The positive current through the resistor of the bottom loop becomes a negative current through the top loop.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/opv3cTS.png" alt="Image" width="40%" /></div>

The corresponding circuit diagram looks like this:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//522f3264-ab6b-4121-850a-79aaef04f5ec/markdown_3/imgs/img_in_image_box_268_941_482_1434.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-11T07%3A56%3A34Z%2F-1%2F%2F28e4bafeb2175ee2d3202ecae7281cf649bcf84e6dbe8aa27e8dd9c6c42e4962" alt="Image" width="20%" /></div>


Notice that the two dots are drawn on **opposite ends** to indicate the **opposite coupling**.

One more thing: You can couple circuit loops at other parts, too. Not just resistors, but also ammeters, switches, capacitors, and even the battery. Here, two circuit loops are **oppositely coupled at a switch**:

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://i.imgur.com/PXYq8SU.png" alt="Image" width="70%" /></div>

There's only one part you can't directly couple to: the junction. That's because it doesn't have any joined sprockets at all. Still, if you need to, you can always connect the junction to another part (like a switch) and couple to that part instead.
