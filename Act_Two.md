#### Quick Review

We covered a lot of ground already! The things you learned are going to be used over and over again, so let's quickly review some of the key concepts.

**Voltage** is the force pushing the chain. Voltage is measured in spin volts, or  V.

**Current** is the speed of the chain. Current is measured in spin amps, or  A.

<u>Types of connections:</u>

<div style="text-align: center;">
    <img src="https://i.imgur.com/PRQqAxR.png" alt="Image" width="60%">
</div>


When parts are connected in series, they are directly connected together. When one part moves, the other part has to move at the same speed. **Parts connected in series always have the same current, but the voltage across each part may be different.**

When parts are connected in parallel, they are connected to separate chain loops, joined by a junction. **Parts connected in parallel always feel the same voltage, but the current through each part may be different.**

## Tutorial 19: Series and Parallel Capacitors

Imagine, for a second: What would a **larger** capacitor feel like? Would it be harder or easier to turn?

The answer is that it would feel **easier** to turn than a small capacitor! Capacitance values tell you how much you can charge a capacitor before it pushes back with a certain voltage. A small capacitor will push back after just a **little bit** of charge. A large capacitor will push back only after a **lot** of charge.

One way we can make larger and smaller capacitance is by putting capacitors in **series** or in **parallel**.
<div style="text-align: center;">
    <img src="https://i.imgur.com/MMtthN4.png" alt="Image" width="40%">
</div>

**Try it out!** First, build the parallel circuit above. Then, turn the switch a half of a turn with your fingers and feel how much it pushes back. Now, hold one of the capacitors still with your other hand and turn the switch the **same distance** as before. Can you feel the difference? **When capacitors are in parallel, their capacitance adds together.**

Next, build the series circuit above and turn the switch the same distance again. It pushes back harder, doesn't it? That's because **capacitors in series make a lower capacitance.**

## Tutorial 20: Capacitor Voltage Rating

Capacitors have another important property: their voltage rating. If pushed beyond their voltage rating, electrical capacitors often break (or explode). My spintronic capacitors just stop turning (or fall over) when the voltage across them is over 9 V.

When I put the capacitors in parallel, I double the capacitance, and the voltage rating stays the same (A). But what if I double the capacitance by spintronic coupling (B)? What is the voltage rating of the capacitor now?

<div style="text-align: center;">
    <img src="https://i.imgur.com/qQNJi4E.png" alt="Image" width="40%">
</div>

It's half of what it was before! With spintronic coupling, we trade a lower voltage rating for more capacitance.

## Tutorial 21: Spintronic Transistors

A spintronic transistor is a voltage-controlled resistor. The top sprocket is the **gate**. It controls the resistance of the bottom sprocket (the **path**).

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//541d3063-3106-4c0d-9d46-76bb106e5437/markdown_1/imgs/img_in_image_box_467_413_883_766.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-13T09%3A35%3A10Z%2F-1%2F%2F24123e8c3841722794a699d7e4d60a422cfd84890a236a7f702673225c9700f9" alt="Image" width="40%" /></div>

Think of transistors like dimmer switches. The more you turn the gate, the lower the resistance of the path. In fact, they work just like in this graph:

<div style="text-align: center;">
    <img src="https://i.imgur.com/IlX95AW.png" alt="Image" width="40%">
</div>

Think about this graph in three separate regions:

<div style="text-align: center;">
    <img src="https://i.imgur.com/HxuqdUa.png" alt="Image" width="40%">
</div>

1. <span style="color: #920c0e;"><b>"Off"</b></span>: When the voltage on the gate is zero and the path resistance is as high as it gets.

2. <span style="color: #be8e1b;"><b>Active region</b></span>: Between 0 V and 1.5 V, where a change in the voltage on the gate causes a change in path resistance.

3. <span style="color: #214028;"><b>"On" region</b></span>: When the voltage on the gate is greater than 1.5 V and the path resistance is nearly 0  Ω.

<div style="text-align: center;"><img src="https://pplines-online.bj.bcebos.com/deploy/official/paddleocr/pp-ocr-vl-16-online//b6008f0c-c0c2-481b-afa8-b2121577577d/markdown_2/imgs/img_in_image_box_403_407_738_800.jpg?authorization=bce-auth-v1%2FALTAKDN8mY5KlNI7zaRpLmOqrw%2F2026-08-13T09%3A34%3A59Z%2F-1%2F%2Fc89e5f839b33615ffd2d338a12a0c0372ee8e258fb68c2bf65550c6f165d8858" alt="Image" width="30%" /></div>


A voltage applied to "In" is amplified into a **higher** voltage at "Out". Can you figure out how it works? It's just a **voltage divider**! But instead of a **resistor** on top, the **transistor's path** is on top. The higher the voltage on the gate, the lower the resistance of the path, and the greater the voltage across the 1000 Ω resistor.

Ok, so why do we need this circuit when we can already make voltage doublers with spintronic coupling? Well, unlike a voltage doubler, this amplifier can supply a **lot of current**. The input takes almost no current to operate, but the output can supply a lot of current (especially when the transistor is fully open)!

## Tutorial 27: Biasing a Transistor

Transistors can do a lot, but one thing bothers me. They're kinda... **inflexible**. They're always off at 0 V, on above 1.5 V, and partway on in between. But what if you need it to turn on at 6 V instead of 1.5 V? Or what if you want it to be on at 0 V, not off? Will I need to make a **custom transistor** for each situation? **Ugh.** That would be a **lot** of work.

Unless...wait, **I've got it!** I can apply a **bias voltage** to the transistor's gate! A **bias voltage** is a constant voltage added to the gate of a transistor. It makes it act like a different transistor. For example, this transistor has a -6 V bias added by the battery. Now, instead of the transistor turning on when the MVS is above 1.5 V, it only turns on above 7.5 V! **Try it!**
